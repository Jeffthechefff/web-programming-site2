History of the Internet

A timeline of important events in the development of the Internet

Internet History Timeline

The Internet developed through many different discoveries, experiments, and networking projects. The following timeline presents some of the major events that contributed to its development.

1957 — Creation of ARPA

In 1957, the Soviet Union launched Sputnik 1. In response, the United States created the Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA) within the Department of Defense. ARPA later supported research that contributed to the development of computer networking.

Reference: https://www.darpa.mil/about-us/timeline

1962 — The Idea of a Global Computer Network

J.C.R. Licklider, a researcher associated with ARPA, described ideas about a globally interconnected set of computers. His vision involved computers communicating with one another and allowing people to access information and resources from different locations.

These ideas influenced later research into computer networking.

Reference: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

1965 — First Wide-Area Computer Network Experiment

In 1965, researchers Lawrence Roberts and Thomas Merrill connected a computer in Massachusetts with another computer located in California. The experiment demonstrated that computers in distant locations could communicate through a telephone line.

The experiment also provided researchers with practical experience in communicating between geographically separated computers.

Reference: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

1969 — ARPANET Goes Online

In 1969, ARPANET connected its first four host computers. The initial locations included UCLA, the Stanford Research Institute, UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah.

This network became one of the most important foundations for later Internet development.

Reference: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

1971 — Electronic Mail

In 1971, Ray Tomlinson developed a program that allowed electronic messages to be sent between computers connected through ARPANET.

He also selected the "@" symbol as part of the format used for identifying the destination of an email message.

Reference: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

1973 — International Connections

ARPANET began making international connections in the 1970s. In 1973, networks in the United Kingdom and Norway were connected to ARPANET.

These connections demonstrated the importance of developing networking technologies that could work across different countries and independent networks.

Reference: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

1974 — TCP is Proposed

In 1974, Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn published a paper describing a method for connecting different packet-switched networks. Their work contributed to the development of the Transmission Control Protocol, which later became part of TCP/IP.

The basic idea was to allow separate networks to communicate without requiring every network to use the same internal technology.

Reference: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

1983 — TCP/IP Becomes the Standard

On January 1, 1983, ARPANET officially switched from its previous networking protocol to TCP/IP.

This transition was significant because TCP/IP provided a standard method for different networks to communicate. It became a fundamental part of the architecture of the Internet.

Reference: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

1986 — NSFNET Is Established

In 1986, the United States National Science Foundation established NSFNET. It was designed to connect researchers and supercomputer centers and became an important part of the expanding academic network infrastructure.

NSFNET later played an important role in the expansion of Internet connectivity in the United States.

Reference: https://www.nsf.gov/impacts/internet

1989 — Proposal for the World Wide Web

In March 1989, Tim Berners-Lee submitted a proposal at CERN for a system that could make information easier to share between researchers.

His proposal eventually developed into the World Wide Web. The Web introduced a system based on documents connected through hyperlinks.

Reference: https://home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/

1990 — The First Web Server and Browser

By the end of 1990, Tim Berners-Lee had developed the basic technologies needed for the World Wide Web, including the first web browser, the first web server, and the first website.

The first web server was hosted on a NeXT computer at CERN.

Reference: https://home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/

1991 — The Web Becomes Available Outside CERN

In August 1991, Tim Berners-Lee announced the World Wide Web software on Internet newsgroups. This allowed people outside CERN to learn about the project and obtain the necessary software.

The Web began spreading to other research institutions and countries.

Reference: https://timeline.web.cern.ch/timeline-header/90

1993 — The Web Enters the Public Domain

On April 30, 1993, CERN made the World Wide Web software available on a royalty-free basis.

This helped remove restrictions on the use of the technology and contributed to the rapid growth of the Web.

Reference: https://home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/

1994 — The World Wide Web Consortium

In 1994, Tim Berners-Lee founded the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C) at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

The organization was created to develop open standards for the Web and help ensure that different technologies could work together.

Reference: https://www.w3.org/History.html

1995 — NSFNET's Backbone Role Ends

In 1995, NSFNET stopped operating as the primary backbone for the Internet. Commercial Internet networks had expanded and were increasingly carrying Internet traffic.

This period marked an important transition toward a more commercially operated Internet.

Reference: https://www.nsf.gov/impacts/internet

2000s — The Growth of Broadband

During the 2000s, broadband Internet connections became increasingly common. Faster connections made it easier for people to access websites, multimedia content, online communication, and other Internet services.

The growth of broadband also contributed to the expansion of online services and digital media.

Reference: https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/

2007 — The Smartphone Era Expands

The introduction of modern smartphones helped make Internet access increasingly mobile. Mobile devices allowed users to access websites and online services without being connected to a traditional desktop computer.

The growth of smartphones contributed to the transformation of the Internet into a service that could be accessed from many different locations.

Reference: https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2007/01/09Apple-Reinvents-the-Phone-with-iPhone/

Today — A Global Network

Today, the Internet connects billions of people and devices around the world. It supports communication, education, research, commerce, entertainment, cloud computing, and many other activities.

The modern Internet continues to evolve as new networking technologies, devices, applications, and services are developed.

Reference: https://www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/

Conclusion

The Internet is the result of decades of development. From early networking experiments in the 1960s, through ARPANET, TCP/IP, NSFNET, and the creation of the World Wide Web, each stage introduced technologies that helped expand global connectivity.

The timeline shows that the Internet was built gradually rather than being invented as a single finished technology.
