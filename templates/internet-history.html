Internet History Timeline

1969 - ARPANET Is Created

In 1969, the Advanced Research Projects Agency Network (ARPANET) began connecting computers at research institutions in the United States. The first four nodes were located at UCLA, the Stanford Research Institute, UC Santa Barbara, and the University of Utah. ARPANET demonstrated that geographically separated computers could communicate over a packet-switching network and became an important predecessor of the modern Internet.

www.nsf.gov/impacts/internet

1973 - TCP/IP Development Begins

During the early 1970s, researchers working with DARPA began investigating how separate computer networks could communicate with one another. Vint Cerf and Robert Kahn developed the fundamental ideas behind TCP/IP, a system of protocols designed to allow different networks to exchange information. This concept was essential to creating an interconnected "network of networks," which is the basic idea behind the Internet.

www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet-related-networks/

1983 - ARPANET Adopts TCP/IP

On January 1, 1983, ARPANET officially switched from its earlier Network Control Protocol to TCP/IP. TCP/IP provided a standardized way for computers and different networks to communicate with one another. This transition is considered an important milestone because TCP/IP became the fundamental protocol suite used by the Internet.

www.internetsociety.org/news/press-releases/2013/new-years-day-marks-30th-anniversary-of-major-milestone-for-global-internet/

1984 - Domain Name System (DNS) Is Introduced

As the number of computers connected to networks increased, it became impractical for people to remember numerical IP addresses for every computer. The Domain Name System (DNS) provided a hierarchical naming system that allowed computers and services to be identified using easier-to-remember names. DNS became an important part of making the growing Internet easier for people to use.

www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

1986 - NSFNET Is Launched

In 1986, the U.S. National Science Foundation launched NSFNET to connect academic researchers with supercomputer centers. It initially connected five NSF university-based supercomputer centers and was opened to academic users beyond just supercomputer researchers. NSFNET eventually became a major backbone of the early Internet and helped Internet connectivity expand significantly during the 1980s and 1990s.

www.nsf.gov/impacts/internet

1989 - The World Wide Web Is Proposed

In March 1989, British scientist Tim Berners-Lee, while working at CERN, wrote his first proposal for what would become the World Wide Web. He wanted to create a system that would allow researchers at universities and institutes around the world to easily share information. The proposal combined hypertext with Internet technologies to create a system of interconnected information.

www.home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/short-history-web/

1990 - The First Web Server and Browser Are Created

By the end of 1990, Tim Berners-Lee had created the first Web server and Web browser at CERN. The first website, info.cern.ch, was hosted on a NeXT computer in his office and contained information about the World Wide Web project. These developments turned the Web from a proposal into a working system.

www.home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/short-history-web/

1991 - The World Wide Web Is Made Available

In 1991, Berners-Lee released the World Wide Web software, including the Web server, browser, and supporting software for developers. In August of that year, information about the Web was announced on Internet newsgroups, allowing people outside CERN to learn about and begin using the technology. The first Web server in the United States was also established at the Stanford Linear Accelerator Center later that year.

www.home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/short-history-web/

1993 - Mosaic Helps Popularize the Web

In early 1993, the National Center for Supercomputing Applications (NCSA) released the first version of the Mosaic browser. Mosaic provided a more user-friendly graphical interface and was later made available for PCs and Macintosh computers. Its accessibility helped accelerate the spread of the World Wide Web beyond the research community.

www.home.cern/science/computing/the-birth-of-the-web/short-history-web/

1995 - NSFNET Is Retired and the Commercial Internet Expands

By the early 1990s, commercial Internet services were expanding rapidly, with private companies building their own network infrastructure and providing Internet access. In 1995, the U.S. National Science Foundation shut down NSFNET's backbone because commercial networks had become capable of providing large-scale Internet connectivity. This marked an important transition toward the commercially operated Internet that people use today.

www.nsf.gov/impacts/internet

2000s - Broadband Internet Becomes Widespread

During the 2000s, broadband Internet connections increasingly replaced slower dial-up connections. Faster connections made activities such as streaming media, online gaming, large file transfers, social networking, and increasingly interactive websites much more practical. This period helped transform the Internet from primarily an information and communication tool into a platform for many everyday activities.

www.internetsociety.org/internet/history-internet/brief-history-internet/

2007 - Smartphones Transform Internet Access

The introduction of modern smartphones, including Apple's iPhone in 2007, helped accelerate the shift toward mobile Internet access. Internet services that had traditionally been accessed through desktop computers increasingly became available through mobile devices. This contributed to the Internet becoming accessible from a much wider range of locations and situations.

www.computerhistory.org/blog/2007-the-iphone/

2010s - Cloud Computing and Mobile Internet Expand

During the 2010s, cloud computing became increasingly important, allowing users and organizations to access computing resources, storage, and software through Internet-connected services. At the same time, smartphones and mobile networks became major ways of accessing online services. The Internet consequently became deeply integrated into communication, education, entertainment, business, and everyday services.

www.nist.gov/publications/nist-definition-cloud-computing
